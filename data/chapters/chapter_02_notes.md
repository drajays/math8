## POWERS WITH NEGATIVE EXPONENTS
**Note ID:** `CH02-sec-powers-with-negative-exponents`

#### POWERS WITH NEGATIVE EXPONENTS

You know that  $ 2^{4} = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 $ (2 is called the base and 4 is called the exponent)

 $ 2^{n} = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \ldots n $ times, n is a natural number.

Let us now find $2^{-4}$ is equal to what?

Observe the following:

 $$ 2^{4}=2\times2\times2\times2=16 $$ 

 $$ 2^{3}=2\times2\times2=8=\frac{16}{2} $$ 

 $$ 2^{2}=2\times2=4=\frac{8}{2} $$ 

Here you can see that as exponent decreases by 1, the previous number is divided by the base 2.

 $$ 2^{1}=2=\frac{4}{2} $$ 

 $$ 2^{0}=1=\frac{2}{2} $$ 

Continuing the above pattern, we get

 $$ 2^{-1}=1\div2=\frac{1}{2} $$ 

 $$ 2^{-2}=\frac{1}{2}\div2=\frac{1}{2\times2}=\frac{1}{2^{2}} $$ 

 $$ 2^{-3}=\frac{1}{2^{2}}\div2=\frac{1}{2\times2\times2}=\frac{1}{2^{3}} $$ 

 $$ 2^{-4}=\frac{1}{2^{3}}\div2=\frac{1}{2\times2\times2\times2}=\frac{1}{2^{4}} $$ 

So, we have $2^{-4} = \frac{1}{2^{4}}$ or $2^{4} = \frac{1}{2^{-4}}$

Thus, we can say that for any non-zero integer $a, a^{-m} = \frac{1}{a^{m}}$ where $m$ is a positive integer. $a^{-m}$ is called multiplicative inverse of $a^{m}$ and $a^{m}$ is called multiplicative inverse of $a^{-m}$.

In general, we have:

If $\frac{p}{q}$ is a non-zero rational number and $m$ is any positive integer, then

 $$ \left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^{-m}=\frac{1}{\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^{m}}=\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{m}=\frac{q^{m}}{p^{m}} $$

## LAWS OF EXPONENTS
**Note ID:** `CH02-sec-laws-of-exponents`

#### LAWS OF EXPONENTS

In the previous class, you learnt that for any rational number  $ a $,  $ a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n} $, where  $ m $ and  $ n $ are positive integers. Does this law also hold for negative exponents? Let us explore. Observe the following:

(i)

 $$ \begin{aligned}2^{-3}\times2^{-4}&=\frac{1}{2^{3}}\times\frac{1}{2^{4}}=\frac{1}{2^{3}\times2^{4}}=\frac{1}{2^{3+4}}\\&=\frac{1}{2^{7}}=2^{-7}=2^{(-3)+(-4)}\end{aligned} $$ 

 $$ \left(\because\ a^{m}\times a^{n}=a^{m+n}\right) $$ 

(ii)

(Note that -7 = (-3) + (-4))

 $$ \begin{array}{l} \displaystyle \Delta^{2} \times \Delta^{3} = \frac{1}{5\sqrt{2}} \times \Delta^{3} = \frac{5\sqrt{3}}{5\sqrt{2}} = 5\Delta^{3} - 2 = 5^{1} \\ \displaystyle \qquad =  \frac{5}{5(-2)} + \frac{3}{3} \end{array} $$ 

 $$ \left(\because\frac{a^{m}}{a^{n}}=a^{m-n},a\neq0\right) $$ 

In general, we can say that:

(Note that  $ -2 + 3 = 1 $)

If $a$ is any non-zero rational number and $m, n$ are integers, then

 $$ a^{m}\times a^{n}=a^{m+n} $$ 

Similarly, we can verify the remaining laws of exponents. So, we have:

If $a, b$ are two non-zero rational numbers and $m, n$ are any integers, then

(i)

 $$ a^{m}\times a^{n}=a^{m+n} $$ 

(ii)

 $$ \frac{a^{m}}{a^{n}}=a^{m-n} $$ 

(iii)

 $$ (a^{m})^{n}=a^{mn} $$ 

(iv)

 $$ (ab)^{m}=a^{m}\times b^{m} $$ 

(v)

 $$ \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{m}=\frac{a^{m}}{b^{m}} $$ 

(vi)

 $$ a^{0}=1 $$

(a) 0 (b) 1 (c) -1 (d) 2

Solution. We will use the properties of exponents to solve this.

 $ \Rightarrow \frac{2^{-2n} \times (2^4)^{n+3}}{(2^3)^{n+2}} = 2^5 $

 $ \Rightarrow \frac{2^{2n+12}}{2^{3n+6}} = 2^5 $

 $ \Rightarrow 2^{-n+6} = 2^5 $

 $ \Rightarrow -n + 6 = 5 $

 $ \Rightarrow n = 1 $

 $ \therefore $ Correct answer is (h).
